How I Learned Python by Building Bots

By Shubhranil Layek

Nobody taught me Python. That is not a complaint, it is just the fact of it. I am a final-year BCA student at Bengal College of Engineering and Technology in Durgapur, under MAKAUT, and I graduate in July 2027. My syllabus has programming in it, the way most syllabi do: enough to pass an exam, write a program that prints a pattern of stars, and forget it by the next semester. Everything I actually use today, the bots, the automation, the AI agents, I learned at my own desk, mostly at night, by building things I wanted to exist.

This post is the honest version of how that went. Not the course list, because there wasn't one. Not the roadmap, because I did not have that either. Just the order in which projects taught me things, what each one forced me to learn, and what I would do differently if a student asked me today where to start. I have been building for two to three years now. I have never held a formal job. Everything below came out of projects, and lately, out of other people's code too.

College Taught Me Syntax. Projects Taught Me Programming.

There is a specific moment I remember. In class we were writing a program to check whether a number is a palindrome. I could do it. Loops, variables, functions, all fine. Then that evening I tried to write a script that would fetch a web page and pull one number out of it, and I sat there stuck for two hours, because nothing in the palindrome program had prepared me for a website that does not answer, or answers with something that is not what you expected, or answers in a format that changes every Tuesday.

That gap is the whole story. Syntax is the easy part of programming, and it is nearly all that college checks. The rest of the job is everything around the syntax: talking to APIs that have their own rules, storing data so it survives a restart, handling the day the internet is down, reading error messages that seem written to insult you personally. None of that is in the exam. All of it is in a project that somebody, even if it is only you, actually wants to use.

So I stopped treating tutorials as the main event. Tutorials became the dictionary I open when a project needs a word I do not know yet. The project decides what to learn next, and it is a much stricter teacher than any playlist, because a project does not let you nod along. It either runs or it does not.

The First Bot, and Why It Was a Telegram Bot

My first real project was a Telegram bot, and if you are starting out, I would push you toward the same thing. The reasons are practical. Telegram gives you the app, the interface and the notifications for free. You write a small Python program, and suddenly the thing you built is sitting on your phone, answering you. That feeling, of code you wrote responding on a device you carry, does more for motivation than a hundred completed exercises.

The bot itself was nothing grand. It answered messages. Then it answered with buttons. Then it fetched things. Each version broke in a new way, and each break taught me something a tutorial had skipped: what an API key is and why you never paste it into your code, what a webhook is, why your program dies the moment your laptop sleeps. I wrote up the foundations properly later, in my step-by-step guide to building a Telegram bot with Python, and it is still the post I send people who ask me where to begin.

One lesson from that first bot stayed with me through everything after it. A small finished thing beats a big half-finished thing, every single time. The bot did one job. It did it while I watched, and then it did it while I slept. Only then did it earn a second job.

One Project at a Time, Each One Harder Than the Last

After the first bot, the projects lined up on their own, because each one left a question the next one answered.

The news bot. I read crypto news in five places and wanted it in one. So I built a bot that pulls headlines from RSS feeds, CoinDesk, CoinTelegraph, CNBC, and posts them to Telegram. It taught me scheduling, and it taught me humility: feeds go down, formats change, and a bot that crashes silently is worse than no bot. I learned to log everything, because a program that runs when you are not looking has to be able to tell you what it did.

The Gemini chatbot, without code. When Google AI Studio made Gemini easy to reach, I built a Telegram chatbot that talks to it, but I built it in n8n, the visual automation tool, instead of Python. Partly curiosity, partly a suspicion that clients would not always want code. It taught me that the thinking behind a bot, what comes in, what gets checked, what goes out, is the same whether you draw it or type it. The full build is in my Gemini chatbot walkthrough.

The price tracker. This one came from being cheated by a sale banner, and it is the project that taught me databases properly. It watches products on Amazon and Flipkart, stores every price it sees in MongoDB, and messages me when a price actually drops. Fifty-plus products, checks running around the clock, retries when a fetch fails. I wrote the whole story in the price tracker post, including the uncomfortable part where the stores do not want to be tracked.

The WhatsApp automation. Telegram is where developers play. WhatsApp is where Indian businesses live. So I built automation on Meta's official WhatsApp Cloud API: webhook auto-replies, a follow-up engine, the full developer setup with the app review and the business portfolio, all of it. This one taught me patience with documentation written by a company that assumes you have a team. The related walkthrough, on getting a WhatsApp alert when someone fills your form, is the friendlier doorway into the same world.

The smaller tools in between. A phone lookup and subscription bot, with admin and user roles, channel-join checks and expiry handling, which taught me that permissions are a feature users feel immediately when they are wrong. A web automation toolkit built on Playwright, Selenium and BeautifulSoup, for login flows and pulling structured data out of pages that were never designed to give it up. And an admission chatbot for my own college, where I hand-rolled a small TF-IDF matching engine instead of using an NLP library, mostly to prove to myself that I understood what the library does.

AgentForge. The biggest one, and my flagship: a framework for building AI agents, the project my GitHub profile is anchored on. It is where everything before it came together, agents that use tools, keep state, and chain steps together. I will not pretend it was planned from the start. It is simply what the ladder led to.

Read that list again and notice what is missing: a plan. I did not sit down in 2024 with a curriculum. I built the thing in front of me, and the thing in front of me kept being slightly harder than the thing behind me. That, as far as I can tell, is the entire method.

The Stuff No Tutorial Put in the Title

Somewhere around the third project, the problems stopped being about Python. The code worked on my laptop. The question became why it stopped working everywhere else.

A bot that only runs while your laptop is open is a demo, not a bot. So came the unglamorous education: renting a small Linux VPS, learning enough Docker to stop fearing it, putting things behind Cloudflare, restarting services that died at 3 AM and reading their logs in the morning like a newspaper. Deployment is the subject tutorials rush through in the last ten minutes, and it is the subject real projects spend half their life on.

Databases were the same story. I started by keeping everything in Python lists, which is a polite way of saying I lost data every time a program restarted. Then SQLite, which is modest and wonderful. Then MongoDB and MongoDB Atlas, when the price tracker needed history that lived in the cloud. Nobody frames it this way, but choosing where data lives is most of what separates a script from a system.

And testing. I will be honest, I resisted it. Tests felt like homework about homework. What changed my mind was fixing a bug, breaking something else, and not noticing for a week. Now my bigger projects carry proper test suites, and I check them the way you check a lock on the way out. Not from virtue. From memory.

Open Source: Where Strangers Reviewed My Code

For a long time my code had exactly one reader, me, and I am a forgiving reviewer. Open source fixed that.

I set myself a simple habit: find one good issue a day in a Python, Telegram bot or AI tooling project, and try to fix it properly. Not drive-by changes. Read the project, understand what the maintainer wants, make the smallest change that solves the problem, write the tests. Some days nothing fits and I report that to myself honestly instead of forcing a bad pull request. Forced contributions help nobody and maintainers can smell them.

So far, five of my pull requests have been merged: into pulse, into agentest, and three into Tadpole, a project whose maintainer, shakewingo, has now reviewed enough of my code to count as an unpaid teacher. One of my favourite moments was being added to a project's contributors list after a merge. It is a small line on a page. It did not feel small.

There is also a pull request of mine sitting open in claude-code-telegram, a project with over 2,800 stars, fixing a security boundary bug where scoped allow rules could bypass the bot's own checks, with regression tests included. Merged or not, that one taught me the most, because security code gets read the way it deserves: suspiciously.

What open source really gives a student is a standard. Your own projects forgive your habits. A maintainer in another country, reading your diff at their breakfast, does not. Every review comment I have ever received made the next project better, including the blunt ones. Especially the blunt ones.

What I Would Tell a Student Starting Today

I am not a guru, I am a final-year student with a GitHub profile. But juniors ask me this often enough that I have a list now.

  1. Build the smallest useful thing first. A bot that does one job and works is worth ten grand plans in a notes app.
  2. Finish before you upgrade. The urge to rewrite your first project in a better way is a trap. Ship it, use it, then build the next thing.
  3. Learn in the order projects demand. When a project needs a database, that week is database week. The knowledge sticks because it has a job waiting for it.
  4. Put your code where people can see it. GitHub is your real marksheet. A profile with finished, documented projects answers questions before anyone asks them.
  5. Read other people's code. Open source issues are free lessons with a test at the end: your pull request either merges or it teaches you why not.
  6. Do not wait to feel ready. I did not feel ready for any project on the list above. I felt ready about halfway through each one, looking back at the part I had already survived.

Questions Students Ask Me

Can a BCA student become a Python developer without a B.Tech degree?

Yes, and I am writing this as one. A degree decides what you are taught. It does not decide what you can build. What worked for me was a public trail of finished projects: bots that run, code on GitHub that anyone can read, and pull requests merged into other people's projects. When someone asks what I can do, I do not argue. I send links.

How long does it take to learn Python by building projects?

The basics took me a few weeks. Feeling comfortable took much longer. I have been building projects for two to three years now, and the honest answer is that each project taught one layer: the first taught the language, the next taught APIs, then databases, then deployment, then keeping things alive. You do not finish learning Python. You just run out of excuses to avoid the next layer.

What should a beginner build first in Python?

A Telegram bot. It is small enough to finish in a weekend, real enough to teach you APIs and error handling, and it lives on your phone, so you actually use what you built. My step-by-step guide to building a Telegram bot with Python is on this blog. After that, build something that watches a thing you care about, like prices or news, and messages you when it changes.

Is contributing to open source worth it for a student?

Yes, for one reason nobody mentions: code review. Tutorials never tell you your code is hard to read. Maintainers do, politely and in public. Five of my pull requests have been merged so far, in projects like pulse, agentest and Tadpole, and the feedback on the ones that were not merged taught me just as much. Start with small fixes in projects you already use.

Do you build bots and automation for others?

Yes. Alongside college I take on freelance projects: Telegram bots, WhatsApp chatbots, automation tools and small websites for shops and small businesses, working from Durgapur, West Bengal. If you have something repetitive that a small program could watch, answer or fetch for you, write to me at subhralayek7@gmail.com and I will tell you plainly how I would build it.

Where This Goes Next

I graduate in July 2027, which is close enough to feel real. The plan is not complicated. Keep building, keep contributing, and turn the project habit into proper work: freelance projects now, and a developer role when the right one shows up. The stack I work in today, Python and JavaScript, Flask and REST APIs, MongoDB and SQLite, Playwright and Selenium, n8n, Docker and Linux, was not chosen from a list of trending technologies. It is just the pile of tools my projects demanded, one at a time.

If you are a student reading this from a hostel room, wondering whether the syllabus is all there is: it is not. Pick one small thing this week. A bot that tells you the weather. A script that renames your downloads. Finish it badly if you have to. The second one will be better, and somewhere around the sixth, you will notice you have become the kind of person who builds things, which is the whole secret, and it was never a secret.

My projects are on GitHub, the walkthroughs are on the blog, and if you want a bot, a chatbot or an automation built for your shop or your work, I am available for freelance projects: write to me at subhralayek7@gmail.com.
